**Slide 1: Title slide.** Introduce the team and the topic. One sentence: we take five years of weekly PM2.5 in Delhi, ask which course forecasting method predicts a full year ahead best, and ask whether wind and humidity help. Everything uses methods from the course: decomposition, ACF and PACF, SARIMA, Holt-Winters, forecast evaluation and the Diebold-Mariano test. The only extra topic is the VAR and Granger test from the handout.

## Problem and Plan

**Slide 2: Why Delhi's PM2.5, and What We Ask.** Why we care: PM2.5 is the most harmful pollutant, and Delhi is among the worst cities. Over the five modelling years the average is 121 micrograms per cubic metre and more than half of all days are above the ninety microgram threshold, the Poor band or worse. For a time series course the interesting part is the structure: values ordered in time, a strong yearly cycle with winter 4.5 times worse than the monsoon, and a level that drifts down. State the three questions. The first compares forecasting methods one year ahead using only the series itself. The second asks whether weather adds information. The third is only a what-if: a model trained before 2020 cannot know about the lockdown, so we use it as a business-as-usual baseline.

**Slide 3: Data and Variables.** Explain the three variables and their units. Wind is the speed of the wind in kilometres per hour, not its direction. Humidity is relative humidity in percent. The weather is a reanalysis for one point in Delhi, so it is modelled weather rather than a station measurement. We use weekly means so that the seasonal period is fifty-two, which the seasonal models need. Two PM2.5 days were missing and were interpolated. Stress that 2020 is kept out of every estimate.

**Slide 4: The Plan: Box--Jenkins and Beyond.** This is the road map of the talk, and it follows the Box-Jenkins procedure from Lecture 29: look at the data and choose a transformation, identify the model structure with ACF and PACF, estimate the parameters, check the residuals and go back if they are not white noise, then forecast and evaluate on data the model has not seen. We fit five models and then combine them into a sixth. Two branches hang off the main line: the weather question and the 2020 what-if. Point to each box as you say it, so the audience knows where we are at every later slide.

## Exploring the Data

**Slide 5: The Daily Series.** Look at the plot. Each winter the thirty-day mean jumps far above its summer level, each monsoon it falls to its lowest. Daily values are very noisy around that curve. The grey part on the right is 2020: the sharp drop in spring is the lockdown, and we deliberately keep it out of all fitting.

**Slide 6: Seasonality by Month.** The boxplot gives the month by month distribution. November to January are far higher than July to September. Note the second feature: the boxes are taller in winter, so the size of the fluctuations grows with the level. That observation is the reason for the log transform on the next slide.

**Slide 7: Why the Logarithm?.** This is the check from Lecture 6. If the seasonal swings grow with the level, the series is multiplicative, and a logarithm turns it into an additive series. We compute the mean and the standard deviation of PM2.5 within each calendar month. On the left the points lie on a rising line, correlation 0.94 . On the right, after the log, the correlation is \ensuremath{-}0.09 . So from here on every model works on log PM2.5.

**Slide 8: STL Decomposition, Seasonal Strength and Drift.** STL splits the weekly log series into a smooth trend, a seasonal component that repeats every fifty-two weeks, and a remainder. The seasonal strength F S is computed from the STL output: one minus the variance of the remainder divided by the variance of seasonal plus remainder. A value of 0.90 means the yearly cycle explains most of what is not trend; the trend strength is only 0.18 . It is an in-sample measure. We fit STL on 2015 to 2019 only, so the lockdown does not bend the trend. The seasonal component alone swings by a factor of about 17 between the best and worst week. One more feature: the annual mean falls by 22 percent from 2016 to 2019, and the number of reporting stations also changed from 10 and 8 to 37 , so part of the fall may be a change in station mix. We do not model this drift, which is why forecasts will turn out to be too high.

## Stationarity and Identification

**Slide 9: ACF and PACF of log PM2.5.** Stationary means the mean, variance and autocorrelation do not change with time. The ACF tells us if that holds. Here the ACF does not decay to zero: it dips below zero at lag twenty-six, half a year, and climbs back to a positive peak at lag fifty-two, one year. That is the signature of a seasonal series, whose mean depends on the time of year. The PACF has two large spikes at lags one and two and little after that, which suggests short-run autoregressive dynamics on top of the seasonal pattern.

**Slide 10: ADF and KPSS: What They Can and Cannot Tell.** Lecture 12 gave us two tests with opposite null hypotheses: ADF has a unit root as its null, KPSS has stationarity as its null. For log PM2.5 ADF rejects with a tiny p-value, \ensuremath{4.8\times10^{-4}} , and KPSS does not reject, so the series passes both tests. That does not mean it is stationary in the sense we need, because the ACF on the previous slide clearly shows a yearly cycle. These tests were built to find unit roots and slow trends, not a seasonal mean that moves up and down every year. So we use them as a check and rely on the ACF to decide that seasonality must be modelled.

**Slide 11: Seasonal Difference nabla_{52} and the SARIMA Choice.** The seasonal difference subtracts the value of the same week a year earlier, from Lecture 20. After doing so the long wave in the ACF has gone and both tests pass. But look at lag fifty-two: the autocorrelation there is now negative. That is a classic symptom of over-differencing, and we meet it again when the seasonal moving-average coefficient comes out near minus one. A further ordinary difference would over-difference more, so d is zero. For SARIMA we compared five candidates that all share the same differencing, because AIC is only comparable between models fitted to the same differenced data. The full table is in the report. The model with one autoregressive term, one moving-average term and a seasonal moving-average term has the lowest AIC, 39.4 , and the next best is 5.8 higher, which Lecture 29 calls substantial.

## The Models

**Slide 12: Overview of the Six Methods.** Before any result, here are all competitors in one table. The first two are benchmarks that use only the calendar. Holt-Winters and SARIMA are the standard course models for seasonal data. Harmonic regression is a regression with autocorrelated errors, from the regression lectures, where the yearly shape is a smooth curve made of a few sines and cosines. The combination simply averages the forecasts, from the last lecture. All of them are fitted to log PM2.5 and the forecast is converted back with the exponential, which gives the median of the original PM2.5.

**Slide 13: Benchmarks: Seasonal Naive vs Seasonal Mean.** Both use only the calendar. The seasonal naive forecast repeats last year's value for the same week, including whatever noise it had that year. The seasonal mean averages that week across all training years, so the noise is averaged out; it is the seasonal index of classical decomposition from Lecture 6, applied to log values. Walk through the example: for the third week of the year the four observed values were 188 , 272 , 238 and 197 micrograms, so the naive forecast is 197 and the seasonal mean, which is the exponential of the average log, is about 221 . The grey lines show how much a single year varies around the blue average.

**Slide 14: Holt--Winters and the Combination.** Holt-Winters is the exponential smoothing method from Lectures 22 and 23. We use the additive version on the log scale, where additive on logs means multiplicative on the original scale, matching what we found for the log transform. The level follows the data with smoothing parameter alpha, and a seasonal index for each of the fifty-two weeks is updated with parameter delta. We estimate the starting values by maximum likelihood, as in the lecture, and let AIC decide on a damped trend. The fitted delta is \ensuremath{7.3\times10^{-5}} , essentially zero, so the seasonal pattern is learnt once and then kept fixed while the level moves. The combination is the simplest idea from the last lecture: average the forecasts of the seasonal mean, Holt-Winters, SARIMA and harmonic regression. Different models make different errors, so an average can be safer, though the lecture notes that it is not guaranteed to win.

**Slide 15: SARIMA.** SARIMA is the main model of the course for seasonal series: a seasonal difference of period fifty-two removes the yearly pattern, and ARMA terms, regular and seasonal, model what is left. At each forecast origin we search a small grid of orders and take the smallest AIC, using only earlier data. Box-Jenkins says check the residuals with the Ljung-Box test of Lecture 29. There is a subtlety: the state-space model that does the seasonal difference starts with a diffuse prior for its first fifty-two states, so the first year of residuals equals the series itself, not an error. If we keep those, Ljung-Box has p-values of zero at every lag and we would wrongly discard the model. Dropped, the residuals pass at lags ten and twenty-six, with p of 0.49 and 0.10 , and fail at lag fifty-two, p of 0.016 , so a yearly pattern is still left. The more important finding is the seasonal moving-average coefficient of \ensuremath{-}0.99 with an enormous standard error: the data cannot pin it down. A seasonal moving-average coefficient near minus one is the standard symptom of over-differencing.

**Slide 16: Harmonic Regression with ARMA Errors.** The second way to handle seasonality: put the seasonal pattern into a regression instead of differencing it. Use sine and cosine terms with the yearly period. With K pairs the curve has only two K parameters, so it is a smooth summary of the yearly shape; the figure shows that K of one is a single wave, K of four already follows the sharp winter peak. AIC chooses K up to ten and the ARMA orders at each origin; it picked 6 to 7 pairs, so the search was not cut off by its upper limit. The leftover errors are autocorrelated, as the PACF showed, so they get an ARMA model, which is the regression with autocorrelated errors from the regression lectures. This answers the question whether a Fourier fit just reproduces the past: it does not, it smooths it.

## How We Evaluate

**Slide 17: Backtest Design: Three Forecast Origins.** This is the train and test idea from Lecture 23, repeated three times. We cannot judge a forecasting model on the data it was fitted to. So we pretend to stand at the end of 2016 and forecast the whole of 2017, then stand at the end of 2017 and forecast 2018, then at the end of 2018 and forecast 2019. At each origin every model is fitted again from scratch using only earlier years, including the choice of orders. The horizon is fifty-two weeks. Be honest here: three test years is a small number, so we are careful with strong statements, and the first test year has only two years of training data, so we treat it as a stress test.

**Slide 18: How We Score: Errors, Coverage and the DM Test.** These are the accuracy measures from Lecture 30. ME is the average error and measures bias; a negative ME means the forecast was too high. MAE is the average size of the miss, in the original units, and is our headline because it is easy to read. RMSE punishes large misses more. All are computed on the original microgram scale after converting the log forecast back with the exponential, which gives the median forecast. We also check whether the ninety-five percent prediction intervals really contain about ninety-five percent of the actual weeks; that is the coverage. With only about a hundred test weeks, a lower MAE could be luck, so we use the Diebold-Mariano test of Lecture 30: for each week take the absolute error of a model minus that of the seasonal mean and test whether the mean difference is zero. Because the errors of a year-ahead forecast are correlated from week to week, the variance uses a Bartlett window of twelve lags, an arbitrary but standard choice. A large p-value means the data cannot tell the two methods apart, not that they are equal.

## Results

**Slide 19: 2019 Forecasts: Naive, Seasonal Mean, Holt--Winters.** First result slide. Each panel shows the 2019 forecast made in December 2018 against what actually happened, in orange. The seasonal naive forecast, a copy of 2018, is jagged because it repeats 2018's random spikes; its MAE is 30.3 . The seasonal mean is smooth, follows the shape of the year and has 27.8 . Holt-Winters has 32.2 and the widest band. In all panels look at the winter peak at the end of the year: the real 2019 peak was lower than the forecast peaks, which is the level drift we flagged earlier.

**Slide 20: 2019 Forecasts: SARIMA, Harmonic Regression, Combination.** The other three. SARIMA has an MAE of 27.5 with a wider band; harmonic regression follows the average year closely, 26.4 ; and the combination, which has no interval, 27.6 . All look like the seasonal mean, because they learn almost the same average yearly shape from the same years. So differences between them are small compared with the year-to-year variation that none of them can foresee.

**Slide 21: Errors by Year.** This bar chart summarises the MAE of every model in each test year. Read it by colour: grey is 2017, green 2018, blue 2019. The tall grey bar is SARIMA in 2017, 72.3 : with only two training years the seasonal difference leaves one year of information and the forecast becomes unstable. Holt-Winters also swings from year to year, 44.7 , 19.2 and 32.2 . The seasonal mean, harmonic regression and the combination stay in a narrow band. The seasonal naive is consistently worse than the seasonal mean.

**Slide 22: The Key Table.** The key table. Each row is a model, with MAE in 2017, the average MAE over 2018 and 2019, the mean error, the Diebold-Mariano p-value against the seasonal mean and the 2019 interval coverage. The combination has the lowest MAE over 2018 and 2019, 23.8 against 24.8 for the seasonal mean, a difference of about \ensuremath{-}0.9 micrograms. That is a small gain and the test does not call it significant at five percent, p of 0.08 . SARIMA, harmonic regression and Holt-Winters cannot be told apart from the seasonal mean either. Only the seasonal naive is significantly worse, p of 0.03 . ME is negative for every model: forecasts are too high on average. Coverage in 2019 is between 81 and 92 percent, below the nominal ninety-five, because the level drifted down and the intervals are too narrow.

**Slide 23: What the Results Mean.** Pull the results together. For question one: nothing beats the calendar average by a significant margin. A year ahead the signal is the calendar, with a seasonal strength of 0.90 , and the short-run autocorrelation has long died out by fifty-two weeks. The combination has the lowest average error but only by about one microgram and the test does not support calling it better. SARIMA ties with the seasonal mean when it has three years of data, 24.3 against 24.8 , and fails with two, 72.3 , which the over-differencing diagnostics explained. The intervals are too narrow in the stress year and in 2019. And none of the models can see slow changes in the level or sudden events, which sets up the 2020 section.

## Does Weather Help?

**Slide 24: The Weather Variables.** Before using the weather we show it. Top is the PM2.5 weekly series, middle wind speed in kilometres per hour, bottom relative humidity in percent. Be clear that wind here is speed, not direction. All three have yearly patterns. The raw Spearman correlations of PM2.5 with wind and with humidity are weak and negative, \ensuremath{-}0.22 and \ensuremath{-}0.26 . Because everything follows the calendar, a raw correlation partly just says it is winter, so we have to remove the seasonal cycle first.

**Slide 25: Remove the Seasonal Cycle First.** Deseasonalising simply means subtracting the seasonal mean, the average of each calendar day of the year over the five years, smoothed over a month. What is left is the anomaly: how much higher or lower than usual that day is for that time of year. Without this step any Granger test would mostly detect that wind, humidity and PM2.5 all change with the season. The bottom panel is the series we use; it no longer has the yearly cycle.

**Slide 26: VAR and the Choice of Lag.** A VAR is the multivariate version of an autoregression: each variable is regressed on the last p days of itself and of the other variables. We choose p with the same AIC and BIC from Lecture 29, so this is not a trust-me choice: the chart shows both criteria for p from zero to ten. AIC reaches its minimum at 4 lags, BIC at 2 . We use the AIC choice, and then check that the residuals are white noise with a portmanteau test, which they pass.

**Slide 27: Granger Causality: Daily Scale.** Granger causality is a prediction question: after using the past of PM2.5 and humidity, does the past of wind still improve the prediction of PM2.5? It is tested by checking whether the four wind coefficients in the PM2.5 equation are jointly zero, with an F-test, the usual test for a group of regression coefficients. Rejecting means the past of wind adds information; it does not mean wind is the only driver. The table shows p-values far below any threshold for wind and for humidity, \ensuremath{<10^{-12}} and \ensuremath{1.2\times10^{-11}} , also when temperature and rain are added. The reverse tests, PM2.5 to wind and PM2.5 to humidity, are not significant, which is physically sensible: pollution does not change tomorrow's wind, wind disperses pollution.

**Slide 28: The Weekly Scale and One-Week Forecasts.** The forecasting problem uses weekly data, so we repeat the question at the weekly scale. First regression of weekly deseasonalised PM2.5 on last week's PM2.5, then the same plus last week's wind and humidity: R squared goes from 0.123 to 0.128 and the F-test p-value is 0.47 , so nothing is added. The same-week weather has R squared 0.17 , but we would not know a week's weather before the week. Then the direct forecasting test: one-week-ahead forecasts from the harmonic model with and without weather. Adding last week's weather, which is what we could really know, makes the error slightly worse, 2.6 percent, and the test says that is significant at five percent: the extra coefficients add noise. Only the same-week weather helps, which is cheating. This reconciles the two results: wind and humidity do change PM2.5 from one day to the next, but over a week the effect is gone, so for weekly or yearly forecasts weather has nothing to add. A year-ahead forecast would also need a weather forecast first. Limits: the weather is a reanalysis at one grid point, and drivers such as stubble burning are unmeasured.

## 2020: What If?

**Slide 29: Why 2020 Is a What-If, Not a Forecast.** Address the obvious objection directly: a model trained on 2015 to 2019 cannot know that a lockdown is coming, so we are not claiming to forecast 2020. We use the model as a business-as-usual baseline. Before the lockdown, January and February, the comparison is a real forecast test. After the twenty-fifth of March, the difference between what happened and the baseline is our estimate of how much lower PM2.5 was than normal. To know whether a gap is large we need a yardstick, so we compute how large the same ten-week gap is in the three earlier years, when there was no lockdown, and in the same calendar weeks of 2019.

**Slide 30: 2020: Forecast and Actual.** Here is the picture. The coloured lines are what the models said in December 2019 for the first half of 2020. The black line is what actually happened. In January and February the black line is somewhat below the forecasts, the same level bias as in earlier years, so the January and February error is larger than in the earlier test years; the models still follow the shape. From the end of March, the lockdown period in grey, the actual values drop far below every model. The ten-week mean was 49 against about 95 for the harmonic model, a gap of \ensuremath{-}49 percent, and all models agree within \ensuremath{-}50 to \ensuremath{-}44 percent, so the conclusion does not depend on which model we pick.

**Slide 31: Is That Gap Unusual?.** This answers whether the lockdown drop is just forecast error. Each grey dot is the ten-week gap for one window in 2017, 2018 or 2019, so it shows how wrong the business-as-usual forecast typically is. The orange dots are the 2020 lockdown weeks. For the seasonal mean, harmonic regression, the combination and the naive forecast they sit below every grey dot. For Holt-Winters and SARIMA, whose 2017 forecasts were poor, a few grey dots reach as low, 3 and 1 percent of windows. The blue diamond is the same calendar weeks of 2019, moderately negative because of the downward drift. Part of the gap is the models' pre-lockdown bias; removing it leaves \ensuremath{-}41 to \ensuremath{-}34 percent, still below ordinary windows for the better models. We call it descriptive, not causal: the weather in 2020 and other events also differed.

## Conclusion

**Slide 32: Answers to Our Questions.** Give the three answers in one breath each. For forecasting: nothing beats the calendar average by a significant margin; the combination has the lowest error, 23.8 , but only about one microgram better, and the standard SARIMA is the least reliable with short data. For weather: it helps over days, not over weeks. For 2020: PM2.5 in the lockdown weeks was about \ensuremath{-}50 to \ensuremath{-}44 percent below the business-as-usual projection, \ensuremath{-}41 to \ensuremath{-}34 after allowing for the models' earlier bias, beyond the usual forecasting error of the better models, but we do not call it causal.

**Slide 33: Limitations.** Be upfront about what we cannot claim. Three test years are too few to rank similar models. We looked at 2019 during earlier iterations of the project, although the code only ever selects using earlier data. The downward drift is not modelled. It is one city, and the weather comes from a reanalysis at one point. And the lockdown gap depends on the assumed business-as-usual level. The repository link has all code, data and results, and the report has the full references.
