**Slide 1: Title slide.** Introduce the team and the topic in one sentence: we take five years of weekly PM2.5 in Delhi, ask which forecasting method predicts a full year ahead best, and ask whether wind and humidity help. The methods are decomposition, ACF and PACF, Holt-Winters, SARIMA, harmonic regression, forecast evaluation with the Diebold-Mariano test, and a VAR with a Granger test for the weather. Finish with a what-if on the 2020 lockdown.

## Problem and Plan

**Slide 2: Why Delhi's PM2.5, and What We Ask.** PM2.5 is the most harmful common air pollutant, and Delhi is among the worst cities. Over the five modelling years the average is 121 micrograms per cubic metre and more than half of all days are above the ninety microgram threshold, the Poor band or worse. The interesting part for a time series project is the structure: values ordered in time, a strong yearly cycle with winter 4.5 times worse than the monsoon, and a level that drifts down. The first question is about forecasting. The simplest forecast is the average of the same calendar week in earlier years, which we call the seasonal mean. We ask how far ahead the standard models can beat that, from one week up to a full year. The second question is whether weather, meaning wind speed, humidity, temperature and rain, helps to predict PM2.5. The 2020 lockdown is a side investigation and not a question we want to answer: it is an outlier by construction, and a model trained before 2020 cannot know about it. We only use the model as a business-as-usual baseline to see how big an unpredictable gap it created.

**Slide 3: Data and Variables.** Explain the three variables and their units. Wind is the daily mean wind speed in kilometres per hour, humidity is relative humidity in percent, temperature is the daily mean air temperature in degrees Celsius and rain is the daily total precipitation in millimetres. The weather is a reanalysis for one point in Delhi, so it is modelled weather rather than a station measurement. We use weekly means so that the seasonal period is fifty-two, which the seasonal models need. Two PM2.5 days were missing and were interpolated. Stress that 2020 is kept out of every estimate.

**Slide 4: Our Approach.** This is the road map of the talk, the Box-Jenkins procedure. Look at the data and choose a transformation, identify the model structure with the ACF and PACF, estimate the models, check the residuals and go back if they are not white noise, then forecast and evaluate on data the model has not seen. We fit three models and two simple benchmarks, and then also average their forecasts. We forecast at several horizons, from one week to a full year ahead. Two side branches hang off the main line: the weather question, and the 2020 lockdown, which is a side investigation and not part of the main comparison. Point to each box as you say it, so the audience knows where we are at every later slide.

## Exploring the Data

**Slide 5: The Daily Series.** How to read the plot: the horizontal axis is the date, the vertical axis daily PM2.5. The thin line is the noisy daily value and the thick line is its thirty-day mean. Each winter the mean jumps far above its summer level and each monsoon it falls to its lowest, so the pattern repeats every year. The grey part on the right is 2020: the sharp drop in spring is the lockdown, and we keep it out of all fitting. Winter averages 217 and the monsoon 48 micrograms. Second point, the transformation. For each calendar month we computed the mean and the standard deviation of daily PM2.5. If the spread rises with the level, the seasonal swings are multiplicative and a logarithm makes them additive. The correlation between monthly mean and monthly spread is 0.94 on the raw scale and \ensuremath{-}0.09 after the log, so from here on every model works on log PM2.5.

**Slide 6: STL Decomposition, Seasonal Strength and Level Drift.** How to read the plot: the four panels are the weekly log series, then its smooth trend, its seasonal component, which repeats every fifty-two weeks, and the remainder. STL was fitted on 2015 to 2019 only, so the lockdown does not bend the trend. The seasonal strength F S is one minus the variance of the remainder over the variance of seasonal plus remainder. The value 0.90 means the yearly cycle explains most of what is not trend, while the trend strength is only 0.18 . It is an in-sample measure. The seasonal component alone swings by a factor of about 17 between the best and worst week. Now point at the trend panel: it drifts down, and the annual mean falls by 22 percent from 2016 to 2019. The number of reporting stations also changed, from 10 and 8 to 37 , so part of the fall may be a change in station mix. The consequence for forecasting is that a forecast with a constant level tends to be too high, which we will see in the results.

## Stationarity and Identification

**Slide 7: ACF and PACF, Before and After the Seasonal Difference.** How to read the plots. The ACF shows, for each lag on the horizontal axis, how strongly the series is correlated with itself that many weeks earlier. The PACF shows the same after removing the effect of the shorter lags. The grey band is where a bar would land by chance for pure noise, so only bars outside it matter. Top left, the ACF of the weekly log series. It does not die out quickly. It starts at 0.87 at lag one, dips below zero around lag twenty-six, half a year, and climbs back to a positive peak at lag fifty-two, one year. That wave is the signature of a seasonal series, whose level depends on the time of year, so we cannot model it as an ordinary stationary series. Top right, the PACF has big spikes at lags one and two and little afterwards. That points to short-run autoregressive behaviour, an AR of order one or two, on top of the seasonal pattern. Bottom row: the same plots after a seasonal difference, that is, subtracting the value of the same week one year earlier. The long wave in the ACF is gone, so the seasonal difference did its job. But now the autocorrelation at lag fifty-two is negative. That is a typical sign of over-differencing, and it tells us to add a seasonal moving-average term to the model. An extra ordinary difference would make this worse, so we use no ordinary difference and one seasonal difference. This is how the SARIMA structure on the next slides is chosen.

**Slide 8: Unit-Root Tests: ADF and KPSS.** Two tests with opposite null hypotheses. ADF has a unit root as its null, so we want it to reject. KPSS has stationarity as its null, so we want it not to reject. If they disagreed we would have an inconclusive result and would have to look closer; here they agree, which is the clear case. For log PM2.5 ADF rejects with a tiny p-value, \ensuremath{4.8\times10^{-4}} , and KPSS does not reject, so there is no unit root and no slow trend that needs an ordinary difference. That means d is zero. These tests are built to find unit roots, not a seasonal mean that moves up and down every year, so the seasonal part is decided from the ACF on the previous slide, which gives one seasonal difference, D equal to one. After the seasonal difference both tests still agree, with an ADF p-value of \ensuremath{1.7\times10^{-4}} and a KPSS statistic of 0.341 .

## The Models

**Slide 9: The Methods We Compare.** All competitors in one table, and what to expect from each. Benchmarks first, because every model has to beat them to be worth using. The seasonal naive forecast repeats last year's value for the same week, including whatever noise it had that year. Its strength is that it needs no fitting; its weakness is that it copies the noise. The seasonal mean averages that week over all earlier years on the log scale, so the noise is averaged out. A worked example: for week 3 , in mid January, the four training years gave 188 , 272 , 238 and 197 micrograms, so the naive forecast is 197 and the seasonal mean is about 221 . We expect this to be very hard to beat a year ahead. Holt-Winters keeps a running level and a seasonal pattern. Its strength is that it adapts as new weeks arrive; its weakness is that with the seasonal smoothing near zero it hardly updates the seasonal pattern. SARIMA removes the yearly pattern by differencing and models the rest. Its strength is that it uses the recent past well at short horizons; its weakness is that it needs several years of history. Harmonic regression describes the yearly shape as a smooth curve. Its strength is that it averages out the noise and is stable; its weakness is that the shape is fixed. The combination averages four forecasts so that their different mistakes partly cancel, though that is not guaranteed. The right column says what each method chooses from earlier data only, at each forecast origin. All methods are fitted to log PM2.5 and converted back with the exponential, which gives the median of the original PM2.5.

**Slide 10: The Three Models.** A plain description of the three models; the equations are in the backup slides if someone asks. Holt-Winters keeps a running level and a seasonal pattern for each of the fifty-two weeks of the year and updates both when a new week arrives. The fitted seasonal smoothing parameter is \ensuremath{7.3\times10^{-5}} , essentially zero, so the seasonal pattern is learnt once and then kept while the level moves; the level parameter is 0.27 . It should do well when the level drifts, because the level keeps following the data. SARIMA works on the change from the same week last year. It then predicts that change from the last few weeks, which is the autoregressive part, and from the last few forecast errors, which is the moving-average part, and it does the same at yearly lags with the seasonal terms. Read a SARIMA with orders one, zero, one and zero, one, one and period fifty-two aloud like this: one autoregressive term and one moving-average term for the short run, no ordinary difference, one seasonal difference, no seasonal autoregressive term and one seasonal moving-average term. We search a small grid of orders and take the smallest AIC, which rewards fit but penalises the number of parameters, using only earlier data. We expect SARIMA to be strong a few weeks ahead, and weaker when there is little history. Harmonic regression puts the seasonal pattern into a regression instead of differencing it: a smooth curve built from K sines and cosines with a one year period. AIC chose K between 6 and 7 , below the upper limit of ten, so the search was not cut off. The leftover errors are autocorrelated, so they get an ARMA model. Its strong point is a stable, smooth seasonal shape, so we expect it to be solid at every horizon.

**Slide 11: Checking the Residuals: SARIMA.** Box-Jenkins says to check the residuals of the chosen model. How to read the plots: on the left the residuals over time should look like noise around zero, and on the right the residual ACF bars should stay inside the grey band. The residuals start in the second year, because a seasonal difference needs one year of history. The Ljung-Box test is a joint test. At lag h it asks whether all of the first h autocorrelations are zero together, not whether the single bar at lag h is. A small p-value says some autocorrelation is left somewhere up to that lag. Up to lag ten and up to lag twenty-six the p-values are 0.49 and 0.10 , so there is no sign of leftover structure in the short and medium run. Up to lag fifty-two the p-value is 0.016 , so once the yearly lag is included something is left; look at the bars close to the orange line at lag fifty-two. The second finding is the seasonal moving-average coefficient of \ensuremath{-}0.99 , close to minus one. That means the seasonal difference removes more than it should, the same over-differencing sign as the negative autocorrelation at lag fifty-two. In practice this makes SARIMA unstable when there are few years of data, which we see in 2017.

## How We Evaluate

**Slide 12: Backtest Design: Year-Ahead and Rolling Origins.** We cannot judge a forecasting model on the data it was fitted to. So we stand at the end of 2016 and forecast the whole of 2017, then stand at the end of 2017 and forecast 2018, then at the end of 2018 and forecast 2019. In the picture blue is the data available and gold is the fifty-two weeks to forecast. At each origin every model is fitted again from scratch using only earlier years, including the choice of orders and K. The horizon is fifty-two weeks. The same models are also evaluated on rolling origins: every week of 2018 and 2019 is a forecast origin, the parameters are re-estimated once a year on earlier data, the model state is updated with each new week, and each origin is scored at one, four, thirteen, twenty-six and fifty-two weeks ahead; the seasonal mean is recomputed at every origin so all methods have the same information. With three test years we make careful claims, and the first test year has only two years of training data, so we read it as a stress test and give most weight to 2018 and 2019.

**Slide 13: How We Score: Errors, Coverage and the DM Test.** These are the accuracy measures. ME is the average error and measures bias; a negative ME means the forecast was too high. MAE is the average size of the miss, in the original units, and is our headline because it is easy to read. RMSE punishes large misses more. All are computed on the original microgram scale after converting the log forecast back with the exponential, which gives the median forecast. We also check whether the ninety-five percent prediction intervals really contain about ninety-five percent of the actual weeks; that is the coverage. With only about a hundred test weeks, a lower MAE could be luck, so we use the Diebold-Mariano test: for each week take the absolute error of a model minus that of the seasonal mean and test whether the average difference is zero. The errors of a year-ahead forecast are correlated from week to week, so the variance is a long-run variance, with a Bartlett window of twelve lags, an arbitrary but standard choice; the formula is in the backup slides. A large p-value means the data cannot tell the two methods apart, not that they are equal.

## Results

**Slide 14: 2019 Forecasts: the Three Models.** How to read the plot: each panel shows the forecast for 2019 made in December 2018 as a coloured line with its ninety-five percent band, the actual weeks in orange, and the seasonal mean as a dashed line for reference. The horizontal axis is the month of 2019 and the vertical axis is PM2.5. Holt-Winters has an MAE of 32.2 and the widest band, SARIMA 27.5 and harmonic regression 26.4 . For comparison the seasonal mean has 27.8 , the seasonal naive 30.3 and the combination 27.6 ; they are in the tables rather than the figure. The takeaway is that all three models follow almost the same average year as the dashed line, so the differences between them are small compared with the year-to-year variation that none of them can foresee. In every panel look at the winter peak at the end of the year: the real 2019 peak was lower than the forecast peaks, which is the level drift.

**Slide 15: Errors by Year.** This bar chart summarises the MAE of every model in each forecast year, for the year-ahead forecasts; lower is better. Read it by colour: grey is 2017, green 2018, blue 2019. The tall grey bar is SARIMA in 2017, 72.3 . The reason is differencing combined with too little history. With only two training years the seasonal difference leaves one year of information, and AIC picked a model with a seasonal autoregressive coefficient of 0.98 , almost one. Such a model forecasts roughly last year plus the change from the year before. The year before that, 2016, was much worse than 2015, because of the November 2016 smog episode, so SARIMA extrapolated the rise: its mean forecast for 2017 was 175 against an actual mean of 124 , with peaks near 1023 micrograms. It is a modelling weakness of seasonal differencing on short data, not a bug. Holt-Winters also swings from year to year, 44.7 , 19.2 and 32.2 . The seasonal mean, harmonic regression and the combination stay in a narrow band. The seasonal naive is consistently worse than the seasonal mean.

**Slide 16: The Key Table.** The key table, for year-ahead forecasts. Each row is a model, with the average MAE over 2018 and 2019, the mean error, the Diebold-Mariano p-value against the seasonal mean and the 2019 interval coverage. Note what the test compares: every model against the seasonal mean, the average of each calendar week over earlier years, and not against the naive forecast. The combination has the lowest MAE, 23.8 against 24.8 for the seasonal mean, a difference of about \ensuremath{-}0.9 micrograms. That is a small gain and the test does not call it significant at five percent, p of 0.08 . SARIMA, harmonic regression and Holt-Winters cannot be told apart from the seasonal mean either. Only the seasonal naive, last year's week copied forward, is significantly worse, p of 0.03 . So no model is clearly better than the plain calendar average a year ahead. ME is negative for every model: forecasts are too high on average. Coverage in 2019 is between 81 and 92 percent, below the nominal ninety-five, because the level drifted down and the intervals are too narrow.

**Slide 17: How Far Ahead Can We Beat the Calendar?.** This is the central picture for question one. How to read the plot: the horizontal axis is how far ahead we forecast, from one week to fifty-two weeks. The vertical axis is each model's average error as a percentage of the error of the seasonal mean, which is the dashed line at one hundred. Below the line means better than the calendar average. At each horizon, every week of 2018 and 2019 is a forecast origin: the model parameters were estimated on earlier data, and the model state is updated with all data up to the origin, and the seasonal mean is also recomputed from all data up to the origin, so every method has the same information. One week ahead every model is below the line: the combination has 82 percent, SARIMA 85 and harmonic regression 87 ; the recent past is informative at this horizon. Four weeks ahead the gains shrink, the combination is at 87 percent. At thirteen weeks the models are at about the same level as the calendar average. At twenty-six and fifty-two weeks Holt-Winters and the combination are somewhat below the line, because they keep following the level while the seasonal mean does not; but SARIMA and harmonic regression are not, and the result at thirteen weeks does not support a steady advantage, so we treat the long-horizon gains as uncertain. The seasonal naive forecast is above the line at every horizon: copying last year's noise is worse than averaging. The takeaway: the models beat the calendar average by roughly ten to eighteen percent up to about a month ahead, and beyond that the calendar average is very hard to beat.

**Slide 18: Horizon Study: Errors and Significance.** The same study as a table, with the average error in micrograms for each method and horizon, and a star where the Diebold-Mariano test says a model is significantly better than the seasonal mean. The last row of the caption gives the number of forecasts at each horizon: the number falls with the horizon, from 104 at one week to 53 at fifty-two weeks, because later targets need an origin early enough. Persistence, which repeats last week's value, is worse than the calendar average even one week ahead and gets much worse as the horizon grows; it is only a reference. One week ahead the combination is at 20.2 , SARIMA 21.0 and harmonic regression 21.5 against 24.8 for the seasonal mean; the p-value for the combination is below one hundredth, and for SARIMA and harmonic regression 0.02 and 0.04 . Four weeks ahead only the combination is significant, 0.03 . At thirteen weeks no model is below the seasonal mean, 25.2 . At twenty-six weeks Holt-Winters and the combination are significantly lower, but they are not at thirteen weeks and the test at long horizons uses few, overlapping forecasts, so its p-values are only approximate. We therefore do not claim a long-horizon advantage.

**Slide 19: What This Means for Q1.** Pull the results together. The answer to question one depends on the horizon. Up to about a month ahead, the models beat the calendar average, because the last few weeks carry real information about the next few: the combination is at 82 percent of the seasonal mean's error one week ahead and 87 percent four weeks ahead. A year ahead, the picture is the one from the key table: nothing is significantly better than the seasonal mean. The signal that is left that far ahead is the calendar, with a seasonal strength of 0.90 , and short-run autocorrelation has died out. SARIMA ties with the seasonal mean on three years of data but does badly with two, which the residual check had hinted at. The intervals are too narrow in the stress year and in 2019 because the level drifted, and no model can see slow changes in the level or sudden events, which leads into the side investigation on 2020 at the end.

## Does Weather Help?

**Slide 20: The Weather Variables.** Before using the weather we show it. How to read the plot: five panels with the same time axis, 2015 to 2019. The top panel is the weekly PM2.5 series, then wind speed in kilometres per hour, relative humidity in percent, temperature in degrees Celsius and weekly rain in millimetres. All of them have yearly patterns: temperature and rain are the most regular. These four are the candidate drivers we test. The raw Spearman correlations of PM2.5 with wind and with humidity are weak and negative, \ensuremath{-}0.22 and \ensuremath{-}0.26 . Because everything follows the calendar, a raw correlation partly just says it is winter, so we have to remove the seasonal cycle first.

**Slide 21: Remove the Seasonal Cycle First.** How to read the plot: the top panel is the daily log PM2.5 in grey with its seasonal mean in blue, the average of each calendar day of the year over the five years, smoothed over a month. The bottom panel is the difference, the anomaly: how much higher or lower than usual that day is for that time of year. Without this step any test would mostly detect that the weather variables and PM2.5 all change with the season. The bottom panel has no yearly cycle left, and that is the series we use for the VAR. We do the same for wind, humidity, temperature and rain.

**Slide 22: The VAR Model.** A VAR is the multivariate version of an autoregression: each variable is regressed on the last p days of itself and of the other variables. Here the variables are the daily anomalies of wind, humidity, temperature, rain and log PM2.5. The number of lags p is chosen with AIC and BIC, computed for p from zero to ten on the system with wind, humidity and PM2.5; AIC reaches its minimum at 4 lags and BIC at 2 , and we use the AIC choice, also after adding temperature and rain. Then we check that the residuals are white noise with a portmanteau test, which they pass, p of 0.17 . The VAR is the model in which the Granger tests on the next slide are carried out.

**Slide 23: Granger Causality: Daily Scale.** Granger causality is a prediction question: after using the past of PM2.5 and of the other variables, does the past of wind still improve the prediction of PM2.5? It is tested by checking whether the lagged wind coefficients in the PM2.5 equation are jointly zero, with an F-test, the usual test for a group of regression coefficients. Rejecting means the past of wind adds information; it does not mean wind is the only driver. How to read the table: each row is a direction, with its F statistic and p-value. Wind, humidity and temperature each have p-values far below any threshold: \ensuremath{<10^{-12}} , \ensuremath{9.7\times10^{-9}} and \ensuremath{4.3\times10^{-9}} , each tested with the other weather variables in the model. Rain does not help, with a p-value of 0.47 . All four weather variables together give \ensuremath{<10^{-12}} . The reverse test, PM2.5 to the four weather variables, is much weaker, 0.015 ; wind and humidity alone, tested the other way round, give 0.12 and 0.62 . So pollution hardly feeds back on the weather, while wind disperses pollution.

**Slide 24: From Days to Weeks: Does Weather Help a Forecast?.** This slide connects the daily result to the forecasting question. We have seen that weather helps over days. But our forecasts use weekly means and look at least a week ahead, so the useful question is whether last week's weather adds anything to last week's PM2.5. How to read the table: each row is a regression of the weekly PM2.5 anomaly, and the second column is how much of its variation the regression explains. The first row uses only last week's PM2.5 and explains 0.123 . Adding last week's wind and humidity raises this to 0.128 , with a p-value of 0.47 ; adding temperature and rain as well gives 0.140 , with a p-value of 0.29 . Neither gain is significant. The last two rows use the weather of the same week, which would explain more, 0.19 with all four variables, but a week's weather is not known before the week, so it can only help a forecast if it is itself forecast. The conclusion: over days weather helps, but its information is used up inside a week.

**Slide 25: One-Week Forecasts with Weather.** This is the forecasting version of the weather question. How to read the bar chart: each bar is the one-week-ahead average error of a model, lower is better. The first two grey bars are benchmarks: persistence repeats last week, and the seasonal mean uses the calendar. The blue bar is the harmonic model using only the history of PM2.5, with an error of 21.5 . The two green bars add the weather of last week, which we could really know: wind and humidity give 22.1 , and all four variables give 22.2 . Neither is better, and the first is slightly worse; the extra coefficients add noise. The two orange bars use the weather of the very week being forecast, which is not known in advance. With wind and humidity that gives 20.0 , a gain of about seven percent that is not statistically significant, p of 0.08 ; with all four variables it gives 22.9 , which is worse than no weather at all, because the extra coefficients are estimated from limited data and add noise. So temperature and rain do not rescue the forecast: last week's weather has nothing to add, and even a perfect weather forecast would add only a little. This reconciles the two results: wind, humidity and temperature change PM2.5 from one day to the next, but over a week the effect is gone. Limits: the weather is a reanalysis at one grid point, and drivers such as stubble burning are unmeasured.

## Side Investigation: 2020

**Slide 26: Side Investigation: the 2020 Lockdown.** This is a side investigation, not one of our questions. The lockdown is an outlier by construction, so we do not try to forecast it. A model trained on 2015 to 2019 cannot know that a lockdown is coming, so after the twenty-fifth of March its forecast is a business-as-usual baseline. Before the lockdown, January and February, the comparison is still a real forecast test. After it, the gap between what happened and the baseline shows how big an unpredictable gap the lockdown created. To judge whether a gap is large we need a yardstick, so we compute how large the same ten-week gap is in the three earlier years, when there was no lockdown, and in the same calendar weeks of 2019. Present it as an investigation of how unpredictable the gap was, not as a causal effect.

**Slide 27: 2020: Forecast and Actual.** How to read the plot: the coloured lines are what the models said in December 2019 for the first half of 2020 and the black line is what actually happened; the grey band is the lockdown period. In January and February the black line is somewhat below the forecasts, the same level bias as in earlier years, by 7 to 17 percent, with a January and February MAE between 28 and 42 ; the models still follow the shape. From the end of March the actual values drop far below every model. The ten-week mean was 49 against about 95 for the harmonic model, a gap of \ensuremath{-}49 percent, and all models agree within \ensuremath{-}50 to \ensuremath{-}44 percent, so the conclusion does not depend on which model we pick.

**Slide 28: Is That Gap Unusual?.** This shows whether the lockdown drop is more than the usual forecast miss. How to read the plot: the horizontal axis lists the models and the vertical axis is the gap in percent between the actual ten-week mean and the forecast, so zero means the forecast was right and negative means the actual was lower. Each grey dot is the ten-week gap for one window in 2017, 2018 or 2019, so the grey cloud shows how wrong the business-as-usual forecast typically is: from about \ensuremath{-}31 to 23 percent for the harmonic model. The orange dots are the 2020 lockdown weeks, at about \ensuremath{-}49 percent for the harmonic model. For the seasonal mean, harmonic regression, the combination and the naive forecast the orange dots sit below every grey dot. For Holt-Winters and SARIMA, whose 2017 forecasts were poor, a few grey dots reach as low: 3 and 1 percent of windows. The blue diamond is the same calendar weeks of 2019, moderately negative because of the downward drift. Part of the gap is the models' pre-lockdown bias; removing it leaves \ensuremath{-}41 to \ensuremath{-}34 percent, still below ordinary windows for four of the six models. We call it descriptive, not causal: the weather in 2020 and other events also differed.

## Conclusion

**Slide 29: Answers to Our Questions.** Give the answers in one breath each. For forecasting: the answer depends on the horizon. Up to about a month ahead the models beat the calendar average, the combination by about 82 percent of its error at one week; beyond that nothing beats it. A year ahead the combination has the lowest error, 23.8 , but only about one microgram better than the calendar average and not significantly. For weather: wind, humidity and temperature help over days, rain does not, and none of them helps a weekly or one-week-ahead forecast. For 2020, which we treat as a side investigation: PM2.5 in the lockdown weeks was about \ensuremath{-}50 to \ensuremath{-}44 percent below the business-as-usual projection, \ensuremath{-}41 to \ensuremath{-}34 after allowing for the models' earlier bias, beyond the usual forecasting error for four of the six models, but we do not call it causal.

**Slide 30: Limitations.** What we cannot claim. The year-ahead test has three years and the rolling study about two, so similar models cannot be ranked with confidence, and the significance tests at long horizons use few overlapping forecasts. The downward drift and the changing station network are not captured by models with a constant level. It is one city, and the weather comes from a reanalysis at one point. A real weather-aided forecast would need a weather forecast, which we did not model. And the lockdown gap depends on the assumed business-as-usual level. The repository link has all code, data and results, and the report has the full references.

**Slide 31: Conclusion \& Acknowledgement.** Thank the instructor and invite questions. If asked about a method, the backup slides give the equations for Holt-Winters, SARIMA, harmonic regression and the Diebold-Mariano test, and the report has the references.

## Backup

**Slide 32: Backup: Holt--Winters.** Backup, only if asked. The level follows the data with smoothing parameter alpha and a seasonal index for each of the fifty-two weeks is updated with parameter delta. Additive on the log scale means multiplicative on the original scale, matching the log transform. The starting values are estimated by maximum likelihood and AIC decides whether to include a damped trend; it never did. The fitted delta is \ensuremath{7.3\times10^{-5}} , essentially zero, so the seasonal pattern is learnt once; alpha is 0.27 . The combination averages the forecasts of the seasonal mean, Holt-Winters, SARIMA and harmonic regression on the log scale; different models make different errors so an average can be safer, but it is not guaranteed to win.

**Slide 33: Backup: SARIMA and the Choice of Orders.** Backup, only if asked. The model is a seasonal difference of period fifty-two plus regular and seasonal autoregressive and moving-average terms. The prediction interval uses the variance of the forecast error from the psi weights. At each origin we search a small grid of orders and take the smallest AIC, which is two times the number of parameters minus two times the log likelihood. AIC is only comparable between models fitted to the same differenced data, so the five candidates all share the same differencing. The model with one autoregressive term, one moving-average term and a seasonal moving-average term has the lowest AIC, 39.4 , and the next best is 5.8 higher, which counts as substantial. The full table is in the report.

**Slide 34: Backup: Harmonic Regression.** Backup, only if asked. How to read the plot: the grey line is the seasonal mean by calendar week and the coloured lines are fitted yearly curves. With K equal to one the curve is a single wave, with K of four it already follows the sharp winter peak. AIC chooses K up to ten and the ARMA orders at each origin; it picked 6 to 7 pairs, so the search was not cut off by its upper limit. The leftover errors are autocorrelated, as the PACF showed, so they get an ARMA model; this is a regression with autocorrelated errors. Because the curve has only two K parameters it is a smooth version of the average yearly shape.

**Slide 35: Backup: The Diebold--Mariano Test.** Backup, only if asked. For each week take the absolute error of a model and subtract the absolute error of the seasonal mean. If the two are equally good the mean of this difference is zero. The statistic divides the mean difference by its standard error; because the weekly differences are correlated, the variance is a long-run variance that adds the autocovariances with Bartlett weights, here up to twelve lags. The choice of twelve lags is standard but arbitrary. We pool 2018 and 2019 and compare every model with the seasonal mean.
