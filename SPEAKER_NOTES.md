## Problem and Plan

**Slide 3: Section overview.** Why PM2.5 matters, the two questions we answer, the data, and the plan we follow (the Box--Jenkins cycle from the course).

**Slide 4: Why Delhi's PM2.5?.** Why we care: PM2.5 is the pollutant most harmful to health, and Delhi is among the worst cities. The average over 2015 to 2019 is about 120 micrograms per cubic metre, and more than half of all days are in the Poor, Very Poor or Severe bands. For a time-series course the interesting thing is the structure: the data come in a time order, they repeat a strong yearly cycle, winter is about four and a half times worse than the monsoon, and the level seems to drift down over the years. These are exactly the features the course tools (decomposition, seasonal models) are built for.

**Slide 5: Our Two Questions.** State the questions carefully. Q1: compare forecasting methods for a one-year-ahead forecast of weekly PM2.5, using only the series itself. Q2: ask whether weather data add information. Q3 is only a what-if: a model trained before 2020 cannot know about the lockdown, so we do not claim to predict 2020; we use the model as a business-as-usual baseline and measure how far the lockdown weeks fell below it.

**Slide 6: Data and Variables.** Explain the three variables and their units. Wind is the speed of the wind in kilometres per hour, not its direction. Humidity is relative humidity in percent. The weather comes from the ERA5 reanalysis for a point in Delhi, so it is modelled weather, not a station measurement. We use weekly means so that the seasonal period is 52, which is what the course's seasonal models need. Two PM2.5 days were missing and were interpolated. Stress that 2020 is kept out of every estimate.

**Slide 7: The Plan: Box--Jenkins and Beyond.** This is the road map of the talk, and it follows the Box-Jenkins procedure from Lecture 29: look at the data and decide on a transformation, identify the model structure with ACF and PACF, estimate the parameters, check the residuals and go back if they are not white noise, then forecast and evaluate on data the model has not seen. Two branches hang off this main line: the weather question in section 7 and the 2020 what-if in section 8. Point to each box as you say it, so the audience knows where we are at every later slide.

## Exploring the Data

**Slide 8: Section overview.** Plot the series, see the yearly cycle, justify the logarithm, decompose with STL (Lectures 04--07).

**Slide 9: The Daily Series.** Look at the plot. Each winter the 30-day mean jumps to 200 to 300, each monsoon it falls below 50. Daily values are very noisy around that curve. The grey part on the right is 2020: the sharp drop in spring 2020 is the lockdown, and we deliberately keep that out of all fitting. That is also why the later decomposition stops at December 2019.

**Slide 10: Seasonality by Month.** The boxplot gives the month-by-month distribution. November to January are far higher than July to September. Note the second feature: the boxes are also much taller in winter, so the size of the fluctuations grows with the level. That observation is exactly the reason for the log transform on the next slide.

**Slide 11: Why the Logarithm?.** This is the check from Lecture 6. If the seasonal swings grow with the level, the series is multiplicative, and a logarithm turns it into an additive series. We compute the mean and standard deviation of PM2.5 within each calendar month. On the left, the points lie on a rising line, correlation about 0.94. On the right, after the log, the correlation is about zero. The Box-Cox estimate is also close to zero, which corresponds to the log. So from here on every model works on log PM2.5.

**Slide 12: STL Decomposition and Seasonal Strength.** STL splits the weekly log series into a smooth trend, a seasonal component that repeats every 52 weeks, and a remainder. The seasonal strength, written F sub S, is computed from the STL output: one minus the variance of the remainder divided by the variance of seasonal plus remainder. A value of 0.90 means the yearly cycle explains most of what is not trend. The trend strength is only 0.18, so the trend is weak. We fit STL on 2015 to 2019 only, so the lockdown does not bend the trend at the end. The seasonal component alone swings by a factor of about seventeen between the worst and the best week of the year.

**Slide 13: The Level Drifts Down Slowly.** One more feature of the data: the yearly average falls from 138 in 2016 to 108 in 2019. We will not model this drift explicitly because a drift term is hard to estimate from only a few years, so later we will see the forecasts are on average too high. A data caveat: the city value is an average over the stations that reported, and the number of stations rose from about ten to thirty-seven, so part of the fall may be a change in the station mix. We list this as a limitation and do not analyse it further.

## Stationarity and Identification

**Slide 14: Section overview.** ACF and PACF, the ADF and KPSS tests, the seasonal difference, and a first look at which SARIMA to fit (Lectures 08--12, 20--21).

**Slide 15: ACF and PACF of log PM2.5.** Stationary means the mean, variance and autocorrelation do not change with time. The ACF tells us if that holds. Here the ACF is not decaying to zero: it dips below zero at lag 26, half a year, and climbs back to a positive peak at lag 52, one year. That is the signature of a seasonal series, whose mean depends on the time of year. The PACF has two large spikes at lags one and two and little after that, which suggests short-run AR dynamics on top of the seasonal pattern.

**Slide 16: ADF and KPSS: What They Can and Cannot Tell.** Lecture 12 gave us two tests with opposite null hypotheses: ADF has a unit root as its null, KPSS has stationarity as its null. For log PM2.5 ADF rejects with a tiny p-value and KPSS does not reject, so the series passes both tests. That does not mean it is stationary in the sense we need, because the ACF on the previous slide clearly shows a yearly cycle. The point to make: these tests were built to find unit roots and slow trends, not a seasonal mean that moves up and down every year. So we use them as a check and rely on the ACF to decide that seasonality must be modelled.

**Slide 17: Seasonal Difference nabla_{52}.** The seasonal difference subtracts the value of the same week a year earlier (Lecture 20). After doing so the long wave in the ACF has gone, and both tests pass. But look at lag 52: the autocorrelation there is now clearly negative. That is a classic symptom of over-differencing, and we will meet it again when the seasonal moving-average coefficient comes out near minus one. A further ordinary difference would over-difference further, so d is zero. This is why in the model section we also include a harmonic regression, which describes the yearly cycle directly instead of differencing it.

**Slide 18: Choosing a SARIMA by AIC and BIC.** This is the identification-estimation step in the style of the Lecture 21 hands-on. We fit four candidates: a plain ARIMA(0,1,1), a seasonal-only model, a combined model with both differences, and a seasonal model with short-run AR and MA terms. AIC trades off fit against the number of parameters, BIC penalises parameters more. The seasonal models are far better than the non-seasonal one, a difference of more than 30 AIC points, which is decisive by the lecture's rule of thumb. Model D is lowest. Caveat from the lectures: AIC across different differencing is only a rough guide, so we also look at the residuals.

## The Models

**Slide 19: Section overview.** All six forecasting methods are introduced here, with their equations, before any result is shown: seasonal naive, seasonal mean, Holt--Winters, SARIMA, harmonic regression and a combination.

**Slide 20: Overview of the Six Methods.** Before any result, here are all competitors in one table. The first two are benchmarks that use no dynamics at all, only the calendar. Holt-Winters and SARIMA are the standard course models for seasonal data. Harmonic regression is a regression with autocorrelated errors, from the regression lectures, where the yearly shape is a smooth curve made of a few sines and cosines. Finally the combination simply averages the forecasts, from the last lecture. All of them are fitted to log PM2.5 and the forecast is converted back with the exponential, which gives the median of the original PM2.5.

**Slide 21: Benchmarks: Seasonal Naive vs Seasonal Mean.** Both use only the calendar. The seasonal naive forecast repeats last year's value for the same week, including whatever noise it had that year. The seasonal mean averages that week across all training years, so the noise is averaged out, which is just the classical decomposition's seasonal index (Lecture 6) applied to log values. Walk through the example: for the third week of the year the four observed values were 188 , 272 , 238 and 197 micrograms, so the naive forecast is 197 and the seasonal mean, which is the exponential of the average log, is about 221 . They are different forecasts, and the second is generally better because it uses all four years. The grey lines show how much a single year varies around the blue average.

**Slide 22: Holt--Winters.** Holt-Winters is the exponential smoothing method from Lectures 22 and 23. We use the additive version on the log scale, where additive on logs means multiplicative on the original scale, matching what we found for the log transform. The level follows the data with smoothing parameter alpha, and a seasonal index for each of the 52 weeks is updated slowly with parameter delta. The forecast for h steps ahead is the current level plus the index for that week. We let AIC choose whether to add a damped trend. In practice the fitted trend option and the smoothing parameters are shown on a later slide.

**Slide 23: SARIMA.** SARIMA is the main model of the course for seasonal series: a seasonal difference of period 52 removes the yearly pattern, and ARMA terms, both regular and seasonal, model what is left. At each forecast origin we search a small grid of orders and take the one with the smallest AIC, using only data before the origin. Forecasts and their intervals come from the forecast function, with interval width growing through the sum of squared psi weights, as in Lecture 28. Set up the tension for the next slide: this is the textbook choice, but it carries a hidden weakness for our data.

**Slide 24: Checking the SARIMA Residuals.** Box-Jenkins says: check the residuals. We use the Ljung-Box test from Lecture 29 on groups of residual autocorrelations. There is a subtlety: the state-space model that does the seasonal difference starts with a diffuse prior for its first 52 states, so the first year of residuals equals the series itself, not an error. If we keep those, Ljung-Box has p-values of zero at every lag and we would wrongly throw the model away. Dropping them, the residuals pass at lags 10 and 26 and are borderline at 52. The more important finding is the seasonal moving-average coefficient: it comes out at minus one with an enormous standard error, which means the data cannot pin it down. A seasonal MA near minus one is the standard symptom of over-differencing, which matches the negative ACF at lag 52 we saw before.

**Slide 25: Harmonic Regression with ARMA Errors.** The second way to handle seasonality: put the seasonal pattern into a regression instead of differencing. Use sine and cosine terms with the yearly period. With K pairs the curve has only 2K parameters, so it is a smooth summary of the yearly shape; the figure shows that K equals one is a single wave, K equals four already follows the sharp winter peak, and all of them are smoother than the seasonal mean. The leftover errors are autocorrelated, as the PACF showed, so we give them an ARMA model, which is the regression with autocorrelated errors from the regression lectures. AIC chooses K and the ARMA orders at each origin. This answers the question whether a Fourier fit just reproduces the past: it does not, it smooths it.

**Slide 26: Forecast Combination and the Fitted Specifications.** The last method is the simplest idea in the last lecture: average the forecasts of the seasonal mean, Holt-Winters, SARIMA and harmonic regression. The motivation is that different models make different errors, so an average can be safer than any single one, though the lecture notes warn it is not guaranteed to win. The table on the right shows what the AIC selection picked at each of the three forecast origins, so you can see that the structure of the models is not fixed in advance but re-selected every time with only the data available then.

## How We Evaluate

**Slide 27: Section overview.** The expanding-window backtest, the error measures (ME, MAE, RMSE, MAPE), interval coverage, and the Diebold--Mariano test (Lecture 30).

**Slide 28: Backtest Design: Three Forecast Origins.** This is the train and test idea from Lecture 23, repeated three times. We cannot judge a forecasting model on the data it was fitted to. So we pretend to stand at the end of 2016 and forecast the whole of 2017, then stand at the end of 2017 and forecast 2018, then at the end of 2018 and forecast 2019. At each origin every model is fitted again from scratch, using only earlier years, including the choice of orders. The yearly horizon is 52 weeks. Be honest here: three test years is a small number, so we are careful with strong statements. The first test year has only two years of training data, so it is the hardest and we treat it as a stress test.

**Slide 29: Error Measures and Intervals.** These are the accuracy measures from Lecture 30. Define each in words. ME is the average error and measures bias, whether we over- or under-predict on average; a positive ME means actual was higher than forecast. MAE is the average size of the miss, in the original units, and it is our headline number because it is easy to read. RMSE punishes large misses more. MAPE expresses the miss as a percentage of the actual. All of these are computed on the original microgram scale, after converting the log forecast back with the exponential, which gives the median forecast. We also check whether the 95 percent prediction intervals really contain about 95 percent of the actual weeks; that is the coverage.

**Slide 30: Is the Difference Real? The Diebold--Mariano Test.** With only about a hundred test weeks, a lower MAE could be luck. The Diebold-Mariano test from Lecture 30 asks whether the average loss difference between two forecasts is zero. We take the absolute error of the model minus the absolute error of the seasonal mean for each week and test whether the mean of those differences is zero. Because the errors of a one-year-ahead forecast are strongly correlated from week to week, we use a long-run variance estimate with a Bartlett window rather than the naive variance. Say clearly what a large p-value means: the data cannot tell the two methods apart, not that they are equal.

## Results

**Slide 31: Section overview.** Forecast versus actual for every model in 2019, then the error, significance and interval tables.

**Slide 32: 2019: The Two Benchmarks.** First result slide. Both panels show the 2019 forecast made in December 2018 against what actually happened, in orange. On the left the seasonal naive forecast, a copy of 2018, is jagged because it repeats 2018's random spikes, and its MAE is about thirty. On the right the seasonal mean is smooth, follows the shape of the year, and has a lower error. In both panels, look at the winter peak at the end of the year: the real 2019 winter peak was lower than the forecast peaks, which is the level drift we flagged earlier.

**Slide 33: 2019: Holt--Winters and SARIMA.** Now the two standard course models. Holt-Winters has the highest error in 2019, about 34. Its fitted seasonal smoothing parameter is almost zero, so its seasonal index is practically frozen at the initial estimate, and it forecasts the winter peak too high; its band is also the widest. SARIMA is the textbook model and in 2019 is as good as the seasonal mean, about 27.5, with a band somewhat wider than the others. We will see in 2017, when only two years of data were available, that SARIMA did much worse.

**Slide 34: 2019: Harmonic Regression and the Combination.** The last two. Harmonic regression follows the average year closely, with a narrower band than SARIMA. The combination has no interval, because we only average the point forecasts. These two look like the seasonal mean, because they learn almost the same average yearly shape from the same years. So their differences are small compared with the year-to-year variation that none of them can foresee.

**Slide 35: Other Forecast Years: 2017 and 2018.** For completeness, the same comparison for 2017 and 2018. Most lines lie on top of each other, which again says all models learn the same yearly shape. In 2017, on the left, the orange SARIMA line and the green Holt-Winters line are far off the others: with only two training years, the seasonal difference leaves only one year of information and the forecast becomes unstable, with an error of 72 against 31 for the seasonal mean; Holt-Winters is also off at 51. This is the main weakness of seasonal methods on short data.

**Slide 36: Errors by Year.** This bar chart summarises the MAE of every model in each test year. Read it by colour: grey is 2017, green 2018, blue 2019. The tall grey bar is SARIMA in 2017. Among the others, the bars are close, and the order changes with the year, which is a first hint that the differences are not systematic. The seasonal naive is consistently worse than the seasonal mean, and Holt-Winters is worse in two of three years.

**Slide 37: Accuracy Table.** The full accuracy table. Each row is a model, with MAE for each test year and, averaged over 2018 and 2019, MAE, RMSE, MAPE and ME. Smaller is better for all except ME, where closeness to zero is better. Point to the winner, SARIMA at 24.3 on 2018 and 2019, but stress the small gaps: the seasonal mean, the combination and harmonic regression are within 1.3. Holt-Winters and seasonal naive are about 29. ME is negative for every model: they forecast too high on average. This ties back to the downward drift: models trained on 2015 to 2018 expect more pollution than 2019 actually had.

**Slide 38: Is Any Model Significantly Better?.** This is the key slide for question one. Each row compares a model with the seasonal mean using the Diebold-Mariano test. The mean difference is how much lower the MAE is, in micrograms. For SARIMA, harmonic regression and the combination the p-values are large, so we cannot claim any of them is better than the plain seasonal mean. The only significant differences go the other way: the seasonal naive and Holt-Winters are worse, with p-values 0.03 and 0.001. That is an honest and useful result: with only the series itself, one year ahead, the calendar average carries almost all the predictable signal.

**Slide 39: Prediction Intervals: Coverage.** Finally, are the intervals honest? A 95 percent interval should contain about 95 percent of the weeks. The table shows coverage by year and the mean width in 2019. Where the coverage is lower than 95 percent, the interval is too narrow, because the model underestimates how much a whole year can differ from the average year, for example because of the level drift. In 2017 SARIMA's interval covered only 23 percent of the weeks: with two training years it was badly overconfident. The seasonal naive covers well only because its band is very wide. This links to the lecture on prediction intervals: the intervals are only right if the model is right.

**Slide 40: What the Results Mean.** Pull the results together and connect them to the earlier diagnostics. SARIMA is not clearly better: with three years of data it ties with the seasonal mean, with two it fails. For a series with a fixed yearly pattern differencing is the wrong tool, since it adds noise by comparing with a single earlier value, and the seasonal MA coefficient at minus one told us that. Holt-Winters suffers from an almost frozen seasonal index. The simple models are hard to beat because a year ahead there is nothing to predict except the calendar: the seasonal strength is 0.9 and the short-run autocorrelation has long died out by 52 weeks. And none of the models can see slow changes in the level or sudden events. That last point sets up the 2020 section.

## Does Weather Help?

**Slide 41: Section overview.** What the weather variables look like, how we remove the shared seasonality, the VAR lag choice, the Granger test, the weekly scale, and a one-week-ahead forecast test.

**Slide 42: The Weather Variables.** Before using the weather we show it. Top is the PM2.5 weekly series, middle is wind speed in kilometres per hour, bottom is relative humidity in percent. Be clear that wind here is speed, not direction. All three have yearly patterns. Winter has lower wind and in the dry season lower humidity. The raw Spearman correlations of PM2.5 with wind and with humidity are weak and negative, about minus 0.2 to minus 0.3. Because everything follows the calendar, a raw correlation partly just says it is winter, so we have to remove the seasonal cycle first.

**Slide 43: Remove the Seasonal Cycle First.** Deseasonalising simply means subtracting the seasonal mean, the average of each calendar day of the year over the five years, smoothed over a month. What is left is the anomaly: how much higher or lower than usual that day is for that time of year. If we did not do this, any Granger test would mostly detect that wind, humidity and PM2.5 all change with the season. The bottom panel is the series we will use. It no longer has the yearly cycle.

**Slide 44: VAR and the Choice of Lag.** A VAR is the multivariate version of an autoregression: each variable is regressed on the last p days of itself and of the other variables. We choose p with the same AIC and BIC from Lecture 29, so this is not a trust-me choice: the chart and table show the criteria for p from zero to eight. AIC reaches its minimum at four lags, BIC at two. The AIC is lower than BIC's choice, we use four, and then check that the residuals are white noise with a portmanteau test, which they pass.

**Slide 45: Granger Causality: Daily Scale.** Granger causality is a prediction question: after using the past of PM2.5 and humidity, does the past of wind still improve the prediction of PM2.5? It is tested by checking whether the four wind coefficients in the PM2.5 equation are jointly zero, with an F-test, the same F-test as for any group of regression coefficients. Rejecting means the past of wind adds information, it does not mean wind is the only driver. The table shows p-values far below any threshold for wind and for humidity, also when temperature and rain are added. The reverse tests, PM2.5 to wind and PM2.5 to humidity, are not significant, which is physically sensible: pollution does not change tomorrow's wind, wind disperses pollution.

**Slide 46: The Weekly Scale: Last Week's Weather.** The forecasting problem uses weekly data, so we repeat the question at the weekly scale. We compare two regressions: weekly deseasonalised PM2.5 on last week's PM2.5 only, and the same plus last week's wind and humidity. R squared goes from 0.123 to 0.128, an F-test p-value of 0.47: nothing added. The same-week weather has a higher R squared, 0.17, but we would not know a week's weather before the week. Reconcile with the previous slide: the daily result is not contradicted. Wind and humidity do change PM2.5 from one day to the next, but over a week the effect is gone, so for weekly forecasts the weather has little to add.

**Slide 47: One-Week-Ahead Forecasts with Weather.** The direct test: one-week-ahead forecasts with and without weather. Each week the model sees all earlier PM2.5 and then forecasts the next week. The baseline is the harmonic model with no weather. Adding last week's wind and humidity, which is what we could really know, makes the error slightly worse, by about 3 percent, and the test says that difference is significant at 5 percent: the extra coefficients just add noise. The only variant that helps is the same-week weather, which is cheating: it uses weather that has not happened yet at forecast time, so it shows the upper bound of what perfect weather forecasts would give. The persistence benchmark, this week equals last week, is much worse than all of them, showing that a model beats naive repetition.

**Slide 48: Weather: What We Conclude.** Summarise so the apparent contradiction is gone. The daily result says weather is useful, the weekly result says it adds nothing, and both are true because they describe different time scales. Weather changes PM2.5 over a few days, and its effect has vanished by the next week. For a one-year-ahead forecast we would also need to know next year's weather, which we do not. Close with the honest limits: the weather is a model product at one grid point, and several important drivers of Delhi's winter pollution, such as stubble burning, are not in the data.

## 2020: What If There Had Been No Lockdown?

**Slide 49: Section overview.** Why a model fitted before 2020 cannot ``predict'' the lockdown, what it still tells us, and how big the gap was.

**Slide 50: Why 2020 Is a What-If, Not a Forecast.** Address the obvious objection directly: a model trained on 2015 to 2019 cannot know that a lockdown is coming, so we are not claiming to forecast 2020. What we do is use the model as a business-as-usual baseline. Before the lockdown, January and February, the comparison is a real forecast test. After 25 March, the difference between what happened and the baseline is our estimate of how much lower PM2.5 was than normal. To know whether a gap is large we need a yardstick, so we compute how large the same ten-week gap is in the three earlier years, when there was no lockdown, and in the same calendar weeks of 2019.

**Slide 51: 2020: Forecast and Actual.** Here is the picture. The coloured lines are what the models said in December 2019 for the first half of 2020. The black line is what actually happened. In January and February the black line is somewhat below the forecasts: the same level bias as in earlier years, about 6 to 18 percent, so the January-February error is 30 to 45, larger than the earlier test years. The models still follow the shape. From the end of March, the lockdown period in grey, the actual values drop far below every model. All four models agree with each other on the business-as-usual path, so the conclusion does not depend on which model we pick.

**Slide 52: How Big Is the Gap?.** The table gives the gap for each model: actual average over the ten lockdown weeks divided by the business-as-usual forecast, minus one. All models give a drop of roughly forty to fifty percent. Two honest corrections. First, in January to March the models were already too high by about 6 to 18 percent, so after removing that bias the lockdown gap is closer to 35 to 41 percent. Second, the last column shows what an ordinary ten-week gap looks like, the 5th to 95th percentile over all ten-week windows in 2017 to 2019: for the harmonic model minus 31 to plus 24 percent. And the same weeks in 2019 show a gap of only about minus 15 percent.

**Slide 53: Is That Gap Unusual?.** This answers whether the lockdown drop is just forecast error. Each grey dot is the ten-week gap for one window in 2017, 2018 or 2019, so it shows how wrong the business-as-usual forecast typically is. The orange dots are the 2020 lockdown weeks. For the seasonal mean, harmonic regression, combination and naive they sit below every grey dot; for Holt-Winters and SARIMA, whose 2017 forecasts were poor, a few grey dots reach as low. The blue diamond is the same calendar weeks of 2019, which is moderately negative because of the downward drift in the level. So for the better models the 2020 drop is larger than any ordinary forecast error. We call it descriptive, not causal: the weather in 2020 and other events also differed.

## Conclusion

**Slide 54: Section overview.** Answers to the two questions, limitations, and thanks.

**Slide 55: Answers to Our Questions.** Give the three answers in one breath each. For forecasting: nothing beats the calendar average by a significant margin, Holt-Winters and the naive forecast are significantly worse, and SARIMA is the least reliable with short data. For weather: it helps over days, not over weeks. For 2020: PM2.5 in the lockdown weeks was about forty percent below the business-as-usual projection, or about 35 to 41 percent after allowing for the model's earlier bias, beyond the usual forecasting error of the better models, but we do not call it causal.

**Slide 56: Limitations.** Be upfront about what we cannot claim. Three test years are too few to rank similar models. We looked at 2019 during earlier iterations of the project, although the code only ever selects using earlier data. The downward drift is not modelled. It is one city, and the weather comes from a reanalysis at one point. And the lockdown gap depends on the assumed business-as-usual level. The repository link has all code, data and results, and the report has the full references.
