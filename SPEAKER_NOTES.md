**Slide 1: Title slide.** Introduce the team and the topic in one sentence: we take five years of weekly PM2.5 in Delhi, ask which forecasting method predicts a full year ahead best, and ask whether wind and humidity help. The methods are decomposition, ACF and PACF, Holt-Winters, SARIMA, harmonic regression, forecast evaluation with the Diebold-Mariano test, and a VAR with a Granger test for the weather. Finish with a what-if on the 2020 lockdown.

## Problem and Plan

**Slide 2: Why Delhi's PM2.5, and What We Ask.** PM2.5 is the most harmful common air pollutant, and Delhi is among the worst cities. Over the five modelling years the average is 121 micrograms per cubic metre and more than half of all days are above the ninety microgram threshold, the Poor band or worse. The interesting part for a time series project is the structure: values ordered in time, a strong yearly cycle with winter 4.5 times worse than the monsoon, and a level that drifts down. State the three questions. The first compares forecasting methods one year ahead using only the series itself. The second asks whether weather adds information. The third is a what-if: a model trained before 2020 cannot know about the lockdown, so we use it as a business-as-usual baseline.

**Slide 3: Data and Variables.** Explain the three variables and their units. Wind is the daily mean wind speed in kilometres per hour and humidity is relative humidity in percent. The weather is a reanalysis for one point in Delhi, so it is modelled weather rather than a station measurement. We use weekly means so that the seasonal period is fifty-two, which the seasonal models need. Two PM2.5 days were missing and were interpolated. Stress that 2020 is kept out of every estimate.

**Slide 4: Our Approach.** This is the road map of the talk, the Box-Jenkins procedure. Look at the data and choose a transformation, identify the model structure with the ACF and PACF, estimate the models, check the residuals and go back if they are not white noise, then forecast and evaluate on data the model has not seen. We fit three models and two simple benchmarks, and then also average their forecasts. Two branches hang off the main line: the weather question and the 2020 what-if. Point to each box as you say it, so the audience knows where we are at every later slide.

## Exploring the Data

**Slide 5: The Daily Series.** How to read the plot: the horizontal axis is the date, the vertical axis daily PM2.5. The thin line is the noisy daily value and the thick line is its thirty-day mean. Each winter the mean jumps far above its summer level and each monsoon it falls to its lowest, so the pattern repeats every year. The grey part on the right is 2020: the sharp drop in spring is the lockdown, and we keep it out of all fitting. Winter averages 217 and the monsoon 48 micrograms. Second point, the transformation. For each calendar month we computed the mean and the standard deviation of daily PM2.5. If the spread rises with the level, the seasonal swings are multiplicative and a logarithm makes them additive. The correlation between monthly mean and monthly spread is 0.94 on the raw scale and \ensuremath{-}0.09 after the log, so from here on every model works on log PM2.5.

**Slide 6: STL Decomposition, Seasonal Strength and Level Drift.** How to read the plot: the four panels are the weekly log series, then its smooth trend, its seasonal component, which repeats every fifty-two weeks, and the remainder. STL was fitted on 2015 to 2019 only, so the lockdown does not bend the trend. The seasonal strength F S is one minus the variance of the remainder over the variance of seasonal plus remainder. The value 0.90 means the yearly cycle explains most of what is not trend, while the trend strength is only 0.18 . It is an in-sample measure. The seasonal component alone swings by a factor of about 17 between the best and worst week. Now point at the trend panel: it drifts down, and the annual mean falls by 22 percent from 2016 to 2019. The number of reporting stations also changed, from 10 and 8 to 37 , so part of the fall may be a change in station mix. The consequence for forecasting is that a forecast with a constant level tends to be too high, which we will see in the results.

## Stationarity and Identification

**Slide 7: ACF and PACF, Before and After the Seasonal Difference.** How to read the plots: each bar is the correlation of the series with itself a given number of weeks earlier; the grey band is what we expect from pure noise. Top left, the ACF of the weekly log series. It does not die out. It starts at 0.87 at lag one, dips below zero at lag twenty-six, half a year, and climbs back to a positive peak at lag fifty-two, one year. That is the signature of a seasonal series, whose mean depends on the time of year, so the series is not stationary in the way an ordinary ARMA model needs. Top right, the PACF has two large spikes at lags one and two and little after that: short-run autoregressive behaviour on top of the seasonal pattern. Bottom row: the same plots after a seasonal difference, subtracting the value of the same week one year earlier. The long wave in the ACF is gone. But the autocorrelation at lag fifty-two is now negative, which is a typical symptom of over-differencing; it comes back when we look at the SARIMA residuals. An extra ordinary difference would make this worse, so we use no ordinary difference and one seasonal difference.

**Slide 8: Unit-Root Tests: ADF and KPSS.** Two tests with opposite null hypotheses. ADF has a unit root as its null, so we want it to reject, and KPSS has stationarity as its null, so we want it not to reject. For log PM2.5 ADF rejects with a tiny p-value, \ensuremath{4.8\times10^{-4}} , and KPSS does not reject. Conclusion: there is no unit root and no slow trend that needs an ordinary difference, so d is zero. These tests are built to find unit roots, not a seasonal mean that moves up and down every year, so the seasonal part is decided from the ACF on the previous slide, which gives one seasonal difference, D equal to one. After the seasonal difference both tests still pass, ADF p of \ensuremath{1.7\times10^{-4}} and KPSS statistic 0.341 .

## The Models

**Slide 9: The Methods We Compare.** All competitors in one table. The two benchmarks use only the calendar. The seasonal naive forecast repeats last year's value for the same week, including whatever noise it had that year. The seasonal mean averages that week over all training years, on the log scale, so the noise is averaged out. A worked example: for week 3 , in mid January, the four training years gave 188 , 272 , 238 and 197 micrograms, so the naive forecast is 197 and the seasonal mean is about 221 . The three models are Holt-Winters, SARIMA and harmonic regression, described on the next slide. The right column says what each method chooses from earlier data only, at each forecast origin. The combination simply averages the log forecasts of four of them. All methods are fitted to log PM2.5 and converted back with the exponential, which gives the median of the original PM2.5.

**Slide 10: The Three Models.** A plain description of the three models; the equations are in the backup slides if someone asks. Holt-Winters keeps a running level and a seasonal pattern for each of the fifty-two weeks of the year and updates both when a new week arrives. The fitted seasonal smoothing parameter is \ensuremath{7.3\times10^{-5}} , essentially zero, so the seasonal pattern is learnt once and then kept while the level moves; the level parameter is 0.27 . SARIMA removes the yearly pattern by subtracting the value from one year earlier and then models the leftover with autoregressive and moving-average terms. We search a small grid of orders and take the smallest AIC, using only earlier data. Harmonic regression puts the seasonal pattern into a regression instead of differencing it: a smooth curve built from K sines and cosines with a one year period. AIC chose K between 6 and 7 , below the upper limit of ten, so the search was not cut off. The leftover errors are autocorrelated, so they get an ARMA model.

**Slide 11: Checking the Residuals: SARIMA.** Box-Jenkins says to check the residuals of the chosen model. How to read the plots: on the left the residuals over time should look like noise around zero, and on the right the residual ACF bars should stay inside the grey band. The residuals start in the second year, because a seasonal difference needs one year of history. The Ljung-Box test asks whether the residuals are white noise, that is, whether any autocorrelation is left; a small p-value says there is. At lags ten and twenty-six the p-values are 0.49 and 0.10 , so the short-run structure has been captured. At lag fifty-two the p-value is 0.016 , so a yearly pattern is still left. The second finding is the seasonal moving-average coefficient of \ensuremath{-}0.99 , close to minus one. That means the seasonal difference removes more than it should, the same over-differencing sign as the negative autocorrelation at lag fifty-two. In practice this makes SARIMA unstable when there are few years of data, which we see in 2017.

## How We Evaluate

**Slide 12: Backtest Design: Three Forecast Origins.** We cannot judge a forecasting model on the data it was fitted to. So we stand at the end of 2016 and forecast the whole of 2017, then stand at the end of 2017 and forecast 2018, then at the end of 2018 and forecast 2019. In the picture blue is the data available and gold is the fifty-two weeks to forecast. At each origin every model is fitted again from scratch using only earlier years, including the choice of orders and K. The horizon is fifty-two weeks. With three test years we make careful claims, and the first test year has only two years of training data, so we read it as a stress test and give most weight to 2018 and 2019.

**Slide 13: How We Score: Errors, Coverage and the DM Test.** These are the accuracy measures. ME is the average error and measures bias; a negative ME means the forecast was too high. MAE is the average size of the miss, in the original units, and is our headline because it is easy to read. RMSE punishes large misses more. All are computed on the original microgram scale after converting the log forecast back with the exponential, which gives the median forecast. We also check whether the ninety-five percent prediction intervals really contain about ninety-five percent of the actual weeks; that is the coverage. With only about a hundred test weeks, a lower MAE could be luck, so we use the Diebold-Mariano test: for each week take the absolute error of a model minus that of the seasonal mean and test whether the average difference is zero. The errors of a year-ahead forecast are correlated from week to week, so the variance is a long-run variance, with a Bartlett window of twelve lags, an arbitrary but standard choice; the formula is in the backup slides. A large p-value means the data cannot tell the two methods apart, not that they are equal.

## Results

**Slide 14: 2019 Forecasts: the Three Models.** How to read the plot: each panel shows the forecast for 2019 made in December 2018 as a coloured line with its ninety-five percent band, the actual weeks in orange, and the seasonal mean as a dashed line for reference. The horizontal axis is the month of 2019 and the vertical axis is PM2.5. Holt-Winters has an MAE of 32.2 and the widest band, SARIMA 27.5 and harmonic regression 26.4 . For comparison the seasonal mean has 27.8 , the seasonal naive 30.3 and the combination 27.6 ; they are in the tables rather than the figure. The takeaway is that all three models follow almost the same average year as the dashed line, so the differences between them are small compared with the year-to-year variation that none of them can foresee. In every panel look at the winter peak at the end of the year: the real 2019 peak was lower than the forecast peaks, which is the level drift.

**Slide 15: Errors by Year.** This bar chart summarises the MAE of every model in each test year; lower is better. Read it by colour: grey is 2017, green 2018, blue 2019. The tall grey bar is SARIMA in 2017, 72.3 : with only two training years the seasonal difference leaves one year of information and the forecast becomes unstable. Holt-Winters also swings from year to year, 44.7 , 19.2 and 32.2 . The seasonal mean, harmonic regression and the combination stay in a narrow band. The seasonal naive is consistently worse than the seasonal mean.

**Slide 16: The Key Table.** The key table. Each row is a model, with the average MAE over 2018 and 2019, the mean error, the Diebold-Mariano p-value against the seasonal mean and the 2019 interval coverage. The combination has the lowest MAE, 23.8 against 24.8 for the seasonal mean, a difference of about \ensuremath{-}0.9 micrograms. That is a small gain and the test does not call it significant at five percent, p of 0.08 . SARIMA, harmonic regression and Holt-Winters cannot be told apart from the seasonal mean either. Only the seasonal naive is significantly worse, p of 0.03 . ME is negative for every model: forecasts are too high on average. Coverage in 2019 is between 81 and 92 percent, below the nominal ninety-five, because the level drifted down and the intervals are too narrow.

**Slide 17: What the Results Mean.** Pull the results together. For question one: nothing beats the calendar average by a significant margin. A year ahead the signal is the calendar, with a seasonal strength of 0.90 , and the short-run autocorrelation has long died out by fifty-two weeks. The combination has the lowest average error but only by about one microgram and the test does not support calling it better. SARIMA ties with the seasonal mean on three years of data, 24.3 against 24.8 , but does badly with two training years, 72.3 , which the residual check had hinted at. The intervals are too narrow in the stress year and in 2019 because the level drifted. And none of the models can see slow changes in the level or sudden events, which leads into the 2020 section.

## Does Weather Help?

**Slide 18: The Weather Variables.** Before using the weather we show it. How to read the plot: the top panel is the weekly PM2.5 series, the middle panel wind speed in kilometres per hour and the bottom panel relative humidity in percent, all from 2015 to 2019. All three have yearly patterns. The raw Spearman correlations of PM2.5 with wind and with humidity are weak and negative, \ensuremath{-}0.22 and \ensuremath{-}0.26 . Because everything follows the calendar, a raw correlation partly just says it is winter, so we have to remove the seasonal cycle first.

**Slide 19: Remove the Seasonal Cycle First.** How to read the plot: the top panel is the daily log PM2.5 in grey with its seasonal mean in blue, the average of each calendar day of the year over the five years, smoothed over a month. The bottom panel is the difference, the anomaly: how much higher or lower than usual that day is for that time of year. Without this step any test would mostly detect that wind, humidity and PM2.5 all change with the season. The bottom panel has no yearly cycle left, and that is the series we use for the VAR. We do the same for wind and humidity.

**Slide 20: The VAR Model.** A VAR is the multivariate version of an autoregression: each variable is regressed on the last p days of itself and of the other variables. Here the three variables are the daily anomalies of wind, humidity and log PM2.5. The number of lags p is chosen with AIC and BIC, which we computed for p from zero to ten; AIC reaches its minimum at 4 lags and BIC at 2 , and we use the AIC choice. Then we check that the residuals are white noise with a portmanteau test, which they pass, p of 0.17 . The VAR is the model in which the Granger tests on the next slide are carried out.

**Slide 21: Granger Causality: Daily Scale.** Granger causality is a prediction question: after using the past of PM2.5 and humidity, does the past of wind still improve the prediction of PM2.5? It is tested by checking whether the four wind coefficients in the PM2.5 equation are jointly zero, with an F-test, the usual test for a group of regression coefficients. Rejecting means the past of wind adds information; it does not mean wind is the only driver. How to read the table: each row is a direction, with its F statistic and p-value. The p-values for wind and for humidity are far below any threshold, \ensuremath{<10^{-12}} and \ensuremath{1.2\times10^{-11}} , also when temperature and rain are added. The reverse tests, PM2.5 to wind and PM2.5 to humidity, are not significant, which is physically sensible: pollution does not change tomorrow's wind, while wind disperses pollution.

**Slide 22: The Weekly Scale and One-Week Forecasts.** The forecasting problem uses weekly data, so we repeat the question at the weekly scale. First regress weekly deseasonalised PM2.5 on last week's PM2.5, then add last week's wind and humidity: R squared goes from 0.123 to 0.128 and the F-test p-value is 0.47 , so nothing is added. The same-week weather has R squared 0.17 , but a week's weather is not known before the week. Then the direct forecasting test. How to read the bar chart: each bar is the one-week-ahead MAE of a model, lower is better; persistence repeats last week and is the weakest. The harmonic model with no weather has 21.5 ; adding last week's weather, which we could really know, makes it slightly worse, 22.1 , and the test says that is significant at five percent: the extra coefficients add noise. Only the same-week weather, 20.0 , helps, and it is not known in advance. This reconciles the two results: wind and humidity change PM2.5 from one day to the next, but over a week the effect is gone, so for weekly or yearly forecasts weather has nothing to add. A year-ahead forecast would also need a weather forecast first. Limits: the weather is a reanalysis at one grid point, and drivers such as stubble burning are unmeasured.

## 2020: What If?

**Slide 23: Why 2020 Is a What-If, Not a Forecast.** A model trained on 2015 to 2019 cannot know that a lockdown is coming, so we are not claiming to forecast 2020. We use the model as a business-as-usual baseline. Before the lockdown, January and February, the comparison is a real forecast test. After the twenty-fifth of March, the difference between what happened and the baseline is our estimate of how much lower PM2.5 was than normal. To know whether a gap is large we need a yardstick, so we compute how large the same ten-week gap is in the three earlier years, when there was no lockdown, and in the same calendar weeks of 2019.

**Slide 24: 2020: Forecast and Actual.** How to read the plot: the coloured lines are what the models said in December 2019 for the first half of 2020 and the black line is what actually happened; the grey band is the lockdown period. In January and February the black line is somewhat below the forecasts, the same level bias as in earlier years, by 7 to 17 percent, with a January and February MAE between 28 and 42 ; the models still follow the shape. From the end of March the actual values drop far below every model. The ten-week mean was 49 against about 95 for the harmonic model, a gap of \ensuremath{-}49 percent, and all models agree within \ensuremath{-}50 to \ensuremath{-}44 percent, so the conclusion does not depend on which model we pick.

**Slide 25: Is That Gap Unusual?.** This answers whether the lockdown drop is just forecast error. How to read the plot: each grey dot is the ten-week gap between actual and forecast for one window in 2017, 2018 or 2019, so the grey cloud shows how wrong the business-as-usual forecast typically is. The orange dots are the 2020 lockdown weeks. For the seasonal mean, harmonic regression, the combination and the naive forecast the orange dots sit below every grey dot. For Holt-Winters and SARIMA, whose 2017 forecasts were poor, a few grey dots reach as low: 3 and 1 percent of windows. The blue diamond is the same calendar weeks of 2019, moderately negative because of the downward drift. Part of the gap is the models' pre-lockdown bias; removing it leaves \ensuremath{-}41 to \ensuremath{-}34 percent, still below ordinary windows for four of the six models. We call it descriptive, not causal: the weather in 2020 and other events also differed.

## Conclusion

**Slide 26: Answers to Our Questions.** Give the three answers in one breath each. For forecasting: nothing beats the calendar average by a significant margin; the combination has the lowest error, 23.8 , but only about one microgram better, and SARIMA is the least reliable with short data. For weather: it helps over days, not over weeks. For 2020: PM2.5 in the lockdown weeks was about \ensuremath{-}50 to \ensuremath{-}44 percent below the business-as-usual projection, \ensuremath{-}41 to \ensuremath{-}34 after allowing for the models' earlier bias, beyond the usual forecasting error for four of the six models, but we do not call it causal.

**Slide 27: Limitations.** What we cannot claim. Three test years are too few to rank similar models. The downward drift and the changing station network are not captured by models with a constant level. It is one city, and the weather comes from a reanalysis at one point. And the lockdown gap depends on the assumed business-as-usual level. The repository link has all code, data and results, and the report has the full references.

## Backup

**Slide 29: Backup: Holt--Winters.** Backup, only if asked. The level follows the data with smoothing parameter alpha and a seasonal index for each of the fifty-two weeks is updated with parameter delta. Additive on the log scale means multiplicative on the original scale, matching the log transform. The starting values are estimated by maximum likelihood and AIC decides whether to include a damped trend; it never did. The fitted delta is \ensuremath{7.3\times10^{-5}} , essentially zero, so the seasonal pattern is learnt once; alpha is 0.27 . The combination averages the forecasts of the seasonal mean, Holt-Winters, SARIMA and harmonic regression on the log scale; different models make different errors so an average can be safer, but it is not guaranteed to win.

**Slide 30: Backup: SARIMA and the Choice of Orders.** Backup, only if asked. The model is a seasonal difference of period fifty-two plus regular and seasonal autoregressive and moving-average terms. The prediction interval uses the variance of the forecast error from the psi weights. At each origin we search a small grid of orders and take the smallest AIC, which is two times the number of parameters minus two times the log likelihood. AIC is only comparable between models fitted to the same differenced data, so the five candidates all share the same differencing. The model with one autoregressive term, one moving-average term and a seasonal moving-average term has the lowest AIC, 39.4 , and the next best is 5.8 higher, which counts as substantial. The full table is in the report.

**Slide 31: Backup: Harmonic Regression.** Backup, only if asked. How to read the plot: the grey line is the seasonal mean by calendar week and the coloured lines are fitted yearly curves. With K equal to one the curve is a single wave, with K of four it already follows the sharp winter peak. AIC chooses K up to ten and the ARMA orders at each origin; it picked 6 to 7 pairs, so the search was not cut off by its upper limit. The leftover errors are autocorrelated, as the PACF showed, so they get an ARMA model; this is a regression with autocorrelated errors. Because the curve has only two K parameters it is a smooth version of the average yearly shape.

**Slide 32: Backup: The Diebold--Mariano Test.** Backup, only if asked. For each week take the absolute error of a model and subtract the absolute error of the seasonal mean. If the two are equally good the mean of this difference is zero. The statistic divides the mean difference by its standard error; because the weekly differences are correlated, the variance is a long-run variance that adds the autocovariances with Bartlett weights, here up to twelve lags. The choice of twelve lags is standard but arbitrary. We pool 2018 and 2019 and compare every model with the seasonal mean.
